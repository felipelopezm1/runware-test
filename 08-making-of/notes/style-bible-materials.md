## Stone and water

### 1. Wet ashlar wall and its mortar

**Words:** Squared ashlar in regular courses. Every face is dressed with fine diagonal chisel strokes a few millimetres apart, all leaning the same way; the arrises are rounded and chipped. The mortar has eroded back one to two centimetres, so each block throws a thin shadow line along its bed joint. Damp darkens the lowest courses most. Raking light picks the tooling out as fine parallel ridges; light from the front flattens it.

**From:** cellar-masonry-2 *(share-alike)* for the tooling, the arrises and the joints; cellar-masonry-1 *(share-alike)* for the courses and how a side light falls off along a wall; cellar-masonry-3 for how a vaulted cellar reads as a room.

**Use in:** storeroom master plate; storeroom plate 4 (low, toward the barrels); all four corridor plates; any wall seen close in shots 1A to 2C.

**Mind:** Our ashlar is dark grey, in blocks 60 by 40 cm, with mortar black with damp. The reference stone is buff to red-grey sandstone with pale eroded mortar (cellar-masonry-1, -2) and pale dry limestone rubble (cellar-masonry-3): take no colour and no block size from them. The reference walls are damp, not running: the thin lines of water down the storeroom walls and the weeping of the corridor come from the LOCK BLOCKS alone. The masons' marks cut into one block are the reference's own: leave them out.

### 2. Moss, lichen and algae on damp stone

**Words:** Growth follows the water. A thin grey-green film lies on the stone where damp sits, with a slightly brighter line in the bed joints. Dull moss furs the lowest courses, heaviest in the bottom half metre, and thin streaks run straight down from each point where water enters. Drier faces carry flat pale blotches of lichen. All of it is matt and swallows light, so the wet stone beside it looks the shinier.

**From:** cellar-masonry-1 *(share-alike)* for the streaks down a pier and the moss on the low courses; cellar-masonry-2 *(share-alike)* for the film and the line in the joints; vaulted-corridor-1 *(share-alike)* for moss where water seeps; roofless-hall-night-2, and roofless-hall-night-3 *(share-alike)*, for lichen blotches in the open air.

**Use in:** storeroom master plate; corridor plates; hall master plate and hall rain plate.

**Mind:** The storeroom block puts "moss and pale lichen in the joints", and the scene's words add "pale green-grey lichen, dripping moss in the vault ribs": keep the growth thin and in the joints and ribs. The references are far greener, with vivid green streaks on a pier (cellar-masonry-1) and whole walls filmed green (vaulted-corridor-1), because daylight reaches them. Do not take that green or that cover into a cellar lit by one lantern. In the hall the block's words are "blotched grey and ochre with lichen, black water stains": use those colour words, not these.

### 10. Poison-dart frog skin

**Words:** Paste the first paragraph with one colour line (49 words, plus 22 to 30).

The skin is smooth and finely granular like wet glove leather, under a thin film of water that gathers as a bright line along the flank. Highlights are small hard glints along the limbs, not a broad shine. The toes are long and thin, ending in small pale discs.

- Electric blue kind: uneven round black spots, none larger than the eye, scatter over head and back and break into fine speckle on the flanks; the limbs carry almost none.
- Acid yellow kind: three broad yellow bands cross head, mid-back and rump between two wide solid black bands; the yellow carries round black blotches and is satin, the black patent-glossy.
- Blood orange kind: head and body are glossy as if lacquered, with sparse small black flecks that thicken toward the rump; broad soft highlights lie on the wet back.
- Lime green kind: the green is matt and finely pebbled; large round black patches sit on flank, back and thighs, glossy as wet enamel, each with its own soft highlight.
- Violet kind: where violet meets black the edge is never clean but a lace of fine lines, linked rings and broken squiggles.
